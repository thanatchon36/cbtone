# Literature cluster b: LLMs for macro/finance text; LLM reliability; training-data contamination

Twelve sources, each located through web search; title, authors, year and venue were checked against the arXiv, SSRN, NBER, IMF, Federal Reserve or PNAS record. Numbers below come from the cited version, and several papers have multiple versions with different headline claims. BibTeX entries are in `refs_b.bib`.

## 1. Lopez-Lira & Tang (`lopezlira2023chatgpt`)

*Can ChatGPT Forecast Stock Price Movements? Return Predictability and Large Language Models*, arXiv:2304.07619 (v6, Oct 2025).

The paper asks whether LLM-scored news headlines predict stock returns. The data are 159,137 firm-headline observations on 4,123 US stocks, October 2021 to May 2024, chosen to fall after the models' training cutoffs. The method is a zero-shot GPT-4 YES/NO/UNKNOWN prompt at temperature 0, compared with 11 other models including BERT, FinBERT, GPT-3.5 and Llama 2. GPT-4 scores track the initial market reaction (portfolio-day hit rate 93.3% overnight, 88.8% intraday) and the subsequent drift (hit rate 58% overnight, 55% intraday). The drift Sharpe ratio is 2.97 overnight. It fell from 6.54 in 2021Q4 to 1.22 in Jan-May 2024. Older models show negative or near-zero drift. For cbtone, this is the canonical "zero-shot LLM scores text and predicts markets" design. It also shows that effect sizes decay as adoption grows, which matters when comparing our tone-surprise results with the literature.

## 2. Hansen & Kazinnik (`hansen2023fedspeak`)

*Can ChatGPT Decipher Fedspeak?*, Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond / SSRN 4399406 (Aug 2023 version cited).

The question is whether GPT models classify FOMC policy stance as humans do. The data are 500 FOMC-statement sentences drawn uniformly at random, each labelled by three reviewers on a five-point scale from dovish to hawkish. The comparison is zero-shot GPT-3 (text-davinci-003), BERT, and the Loughran-McDonald, Henry and NRC dictionaries. Zero-shot GPT-3 reaches accuracy 0.37 and kappa 0.18, against BERT 0.25 and 0.03, LM 0.28 and 0.07, Henry 0.35 and 0.08, and NRC 0.11 and -0.04. Its MAE is 0.41 (BERT 0.66). On a 100-sentence test set after fine-tuning, GPT-3 reaches accuracy 0.61, kappa 0.46 and MAE 0.23. The paper also compares GPT-3.5/GPT-4 explanations with a research assistant's and applies GPT-4 to Romer-Romer narrative shocks. For cbtone, this is the closest benchmark for single-bank (Fed), single-language, sentence-level stance scoring. It is a useful reference for what "good" agreement looks like: five-class kappa below 0.5 even after fine-tuning.

## 3. Bybee (`bybee2023surveying`)

*Surveying Generative AI's Economic Expectations*, arXiv:2305.02823 (May 2023).

This is the LLM-for-macro paper in the Bybee line. (The Bybee-Kelly-Manela-Xiu news-topic paper uses topic models, not LLMs, so it is not used here.) The question is whether an LLM prompted with news can reproduce survey expectations of macro and financial variables. The data are Wall Street Journal articles from 1984 to 2021, with GPT-based forecasts compared against the Survey of Professional Forecasters, the AAII survey and the Duke CFO Survey. The LLM forecasts closely track those surveys, reproduce under-reaction in macro forecasts, and give extrapolative return expectations that correlate negatively with realised returns. On articles published after the training period, agreement with the surveys persists, which the author reads as generalisation rather than memorisation. The abstract reports no single correlation figure. For cbtone, this is a precedent for treating LLM output as a measured belief series. It is also a contamination-aware design, since it uses a post-training holdout, which we can mimic with post-cutoff meetings.

## 4. Glasserman & Lin (`glasserman2024lookahead`)

*Assessing Look-Ahead Bias in Stock Return Predictions Generated By GPT Sentiment Analysis*, The Journal of Financial Data Science (2024); arXiv:2309.17322.

The question is how much GPT sentiment backtests are inflated by look-ahead bias, and whether masking company names helps. The data are 129,431 scraped headlines covering 6,723 firms and 181,908 Thomson Reuters headlines covering 678 S&P 500 firms, with GPT-3.5-Turbo. The sample runs from January 2015 to September 2021 in-sample and from October 2021 to December 2022 out-of-sample. Long-short daily returns on original versus entity-replaced headlines, in basis points, are 25.08 vs 30.97 (scraped, in-sample; p=0.066), 10.74 vs 13.84 (TR, in-sample; p=0.017), 16.32 vs 11.09 (scraped, out-of-sample; p=0.23) and 6.07 vs 12.23 (TR, out-of-sample; p=0.064). Masking raised in-sample returns by roughly 3-6 bps, so any look-ahead bias is outweighed by a "distraction effect" from firm-specific knowledge. For cbtone, entity masking is a feasible control, here applied to bank and chair names. Lopez-Lira et al. (source 9) argue masking is incomplete.

## 5. Sarkar & Vafa (`sarkar2024lookahead`)

*Lookahead Bias in Pretrained Language Models*, working paper (first version Mar 2024; June 2024 version cited).

The paper develops direct tests for temporal lookahead bias, based on events that are unpredictable from a stated information set. It applies them to risk factors extracted from pre-COVID earnings calls, and to US House election winners predicted from candidate biographies, using Llama 2-70B and GPT-4. About 6.8% of generated risk lists from 2019 calls mention "COVID-19", rising to 12.2% in a variant. House winners in races decided by 0.5-2% are predicted with 70-80% accuracy. With the firm name censored, GPT-4 still identifies the firm from a call segment with 70% accuracy, falling to 61% when products are also censored. Prompt instructions to ignore future knowledge only partly help. The authors recommend models whose pretraining data stop before the analysis window. For cbtone, this motivates a documented model cutoff date and a split between pre- and post-cutoff meetings.

## 6. Ludwig, Mullainathan & Rambachan (`ludwig2025llm`)

*Large Language Models: An Applied Econometric Framework*, NBER Working Paper 33344 (Jan 2025; revised Dec 2025).

This paper sets out when LLM output can be used as data in empirical economics. For prediction problems, validity requires no training leakage, meaning no overlap between the model's training data and the researcher's sample. For estimation problems, where the LLM measures an economic concept, downstream inference needs a small hand-validated sample. Without that sample, LLM errors are undetectable and routine choices of model or prompt can shift estimates substantially. It is a methods paper, so it reports no benchmark accuracy numbers. For cbtone, it supplies the design rule that LLM-scored tone should be validated against a human-labelled subset per bank and language. It also supports reporting prompt and model sensitivity as a first-class result, not a footnote.

## 7. Sclar et al. (`sclar2024quantifying`)

*Quantifying Language Models' Sensitivity to Spurious Features in Prompt Design*, ICLR 2024; arXiv:2310.11324.

The question is how much LLM performance changes under meaning-preserving prompt-format changes such as separators, casing and spacing. The data are classification and other tasks in few-shot settings with open models (e.g. LLaMA-2 13B). The method is FormatSpread, which estimates the performance range across plausible formats without model weights. The reported maximum gap is up to 76 accuracy points on LLaMA-2-13B from format changes alone. The sensitivity persists with larger models, more few-shot examples and instruction tuning. The experiments are not on finance or central-bank text. For cbtone, this justifies running several prompt formats per scorer and reporting a range, not a point score. It also suggests that cross-language prompt translation is a formatting confound.

## 8. Gilardi, Alizadeh & Kubli (`gilardi2023chatgpt`)

*ChatGPT outperforms crowd workers for text-annotation tasks*, PNAS 120(30): e2305016120 (2023).

The question is whether ChatGPT can replace human annotators. The data are tweets and news articles (four samples, 6,183 items in the PNAS version; 2,382 tweets in the preprint), in tasks of relevance, stance, topic and frame detection. ChatGPT is run zero-shot and compared with MTurk crowd workers and trained annotators. In the preprint, its accuracy exceeded crowd workers on four of five tasks and its intercoder agreement exceeded both human groups on all tasks. Cost was under $0.003 per annotation, roughly 20x cheaper than MTurk. The PNAS abstract reports about 25 points higher accuracy and around 30x lower cost. Preprint and PNAS numbers differ, so the version matters when quoting them. The tasks are political-science text, not monetary policy. For cbtone, this supports LLM annotation as a valid but needs-validation baseline, and its high within-model agreement is not the same as validity.

## 9. Lopez-Lira, Tang & Zhu (`lopezlira2025memorization`)

*The Memorization Problem: Can We Trust LLMs' Economic Forecasts?*, arXiv:2504.14765 (v2, Dec 2025); SSRN 5217505.

The question is whether LLM economic forecasts in backtests reflect skill or recall of training data. The data are macro series (GDP growth, inflation, unemployment, 10-year yield), market indices, about 4,200 tickers, 90,123 WSJ headlines (1990-2025) and earnings-call openings. The method is prompting GPT-4o (cutoff Oct 2023) and Llama-3.1-70B for realised values. Pre-cutoff, macro MAE is 0.03-0.15 points with over 96% threshold accuracy, rising to 0.26-0.95 points and 29-53% after the cutoff. Headline exact-date recall is 47.0% pre-cutoff and 7.9% post. Anonymised earnings calls were linked to the correct firm in over 85% of cases for all Magnificent Seven firms, and "pre-2010 only" instructions did not stop recall. Masking and instructions therefore look insufficient. For cbtone, this implies treating pre-cutoff meeting scores as potentially contaminated and weighting post-cutoff evidence.

## 10. Silva, Moriya & Veyrune (`silva2025text`)

*From Text to Quantified Insights: A Large-Scale LLM Analysis of Central Bank Communication*, IMF Working Paper WP/2025/109 (June 2025).

This multi-bank, multi-language study asks how central bank communication varies and relates to policy. The data are 74,882 documents from 169 central banks (1884-2025), about 21 million sentences, 87.4% English, then Spanish (6.6%), Portuguese and French. The method fine-tunes a multilingual encoder (bge-m3) on 1,200 labelled sentences. On the 360 held-out sentences, communication-stance accuracy and macro F1 are 0.924 and 0.917, against ChatGPT 4o at 0.828 and 0.570. Sentiment accuracy is 0.700 (macro F1 0.594), while 4o reaches 0.704 and 0.418. Forward-looking net policy sentiment predicts one-period-ahead policy-rate changes, strongest under inflation targeting. Translation-consistency checks cover six languages. For cbtone, this is the closest published precedent for cross-bank, cross-language scoring, and a source of F1 numbers for the "> 2x off literature" check. Note that it uses a fine-tuned classifier plus a GPT-4o baseline.

## 11. Xu & Vilán (`xu2026assessing`)

*Assessing Monetary Policy Globally: Evidence from LLMs and Semi-structural Models*, FEDS Notes, Federal Reserve Board (9 Oct 2026).

The question is whether LLM-scored speeches track a model-based stance across countries. The data are BIS Central Bankers' Speeches covering 119 countries from 1996 to 2025, with figures for six active banks (Bundesbank, Bank of Japan, ECB, Bank of Canada, Bank of England, Federal Reserve) plus Australia. The page does not state the speech languages. Claude Haiku 4.5 scores each speech from -1 (dovish) to +1 (hawkish). Scores are compared with a 244-term dictionary and with semi-structural interest-rate gaps via dynamic factor models. LLM and lexicon scores correlate at 0.49. In several economies the LLM series turns hawkish before the model-implied gap peaks. No factor correlation is reported. As a short note, it is less rigorous than a full paper, and the citation author list is inconsistent on the page. For cbtone, it is a direct precedent for multi-bank LLM tone indices and shows lexicon-LLM agreement is only moderate.

## 12. Jones (`jones2025ornithologist`)

*Ornithologist: Towards Trustworthy "Reasoning" about Central Bank Communications*, arXiv:2505.09083 (May 2025).

The question is whether an LLM guided by human-written decision trees (taxonomy-guided reasoning) can give transparent hawkish-dovish scores with weak supervision. The data are Reserve Bank of Australia communications, in English. The abstract claims the resulting hawkishness and dovishness measures carry information about future cash-rate paths and market expectations, and that the structure reduces hallucination risk. The abstract reports no accuracy or effect-size figures, so none are recorded here. For cbtone, it is a single-bank comparator and a design alternative to zero-shot prompting: a rubric-constrained scorer that could be added as a third scorer type.

## Key numeric benchmarks for the sanity check

- Five-class FOMC sentence stance (Hansen-Kazinnik, 2023): zero-shot GPT-3 accuracy 0.37, kappa 0.18, MAE 0.41. Fine-tuned (100-sentence test) accuracy 0.61, kappa 0.46.
- Multi-bank stance classification (Silva et al., 2025): fine-tuned encoder accuracy 0.924, macro F1 0.917. GPT-4o accuracy 0.828, macro F1 0.570. Sentiment macro F1 0.594 (encoder) and 0.418 (GPT-4o).
- LLM vs lexicon tone correlation, multi-country speeches (Xu & Vilán, 2026): 0.49.
- Masking effect on returns (Glasserman-Lin): +3 to +6 bps/day in-sample, and ambiguous out-of-sample (-5.2 to +6.2 bps).
- Prompt-format spread (Sclar et al.): up to 76 accuracy points.
- Recall pre- vs post-cutoff (Lopez-Lira et al.): macro threshold accuracy above 96% vs 29-53%.

## Gaps

This cluster leaves most bank x language x scorer x evaluation cells untested. Human-label benchmarks exist only for the Fed in English (Hansen-Kazinnik, with GPT-3 and sentences up to 2020), and for a multi-bank set that is 87% English (Silva et al.). There is no published validation of any LLM scorer against human labels for the ECB, Bank of England, Bank of Japan or Bank of Thailand, nor in non-English languages other than the small translation-consistency check in Silva et al. The market-evaluation work (Lopez-Lira-Tang, Glasserman-Lin) covers equity headlines, not central-bank tone surprises or event-study R². Contamination tests (Sarkar-Vafa, Lopez-Lira et al.) use earnings calls, headlines and macro series. None tests whether LLMs recall FOMC, ECB or other policy-meeting outcomes. No source compares prompt-sensitivity ranges across banks or languages, or checks agreement between several LLMs and a lexicon on the same central-bank corpus. Post-cutoff holdouts and entity masking have not been combined in central-bank text.

# Cluster c: Announcement event studies; exchange-rate-based policy (MAS) communication; emerging-market and Asian central-bank communication

Scope: the event-study benchmarks cbtone's "tone vs. market reaction" checks are compared against, plus the thin literature on communication under exchange-rate-centred regimes and in emerging or Asian economies. All entries were checked against bibliographic records or the paper text (see verification notes). Effect sizes are quoted from the papers where the text was read; otherwise stated as qualitative.

## Event-study foundations

**1. Kuttner (2001), `kuttner2001monetary`.** *J. Monetary Economics* 47(3):523-544. Question: do interest rates respond to anticipated versus unanticipated target changes? Data: US fed funds futures, FOMC target changes from the late 1980s. Method: split each target change into expected and surprise components using the futures-implied rate and regress yields on each. Finding: the response to anticipated changes is small; the response to unanticipated changes is large and highly significant, while surprises barely move expectations of later policy actions. For cbtone: the canonical surprise measure we compare "tone surprise" regressions against. Effect size: I could not read the regression tables, so no coefficient is quoted here; see items 2 and 3 for verified magnitudes in the same tradition.

**2. Gürkaynak, Sack and Swanson (2005), `gurkaynak2005actions`.** *Int. J. Central Banking* 1(1):55-93. Question: is the funds-rate surprise a sufficient statistic for policy news? Data: all FOMC announcements, Jan 1990-Dec 2004, intraday (30-minute window) Treasury, futures and S&P 500 data. Method: principal-components rotation into a target factor and a path factor. Finding: two factors are needed; the path factor, tied to statements, dominates long yields. Effect sizes (read from the paper): a 1 pp target surprise gives S&P 500 -4.3% and +49, +28, +13 bp in 2-, 5-, 10-year yields; a 1 pp path-factor move gives +37 and +28 bp in 5- and 10-year yields. Path factor accounts for about 2/3, 3/4, 9/10 of explainable variation in 2-, 5-, 10-year yields. Intraday R-squared for S&P on funds surprise is .36 (daily .12); for the T-bill .80 (daily .56). For cbtone: the template for tone as a "path" factor.

**3. Bernanke and Kuttner (2005), `bernanke2005explains`.** *J. Finance* 60(3):1221-1257. Question: why do stocks react to Fed policy? Data: unanticipated funds-rate changes from futures, 1989-2002 (earlier drafts May 1989-Dec 2001), S&P 500 and industry portfolios. Method: event-study regressions plus a Campbell-style return decomposition. Finding: an unanticipated 25 bp cut is associated with roughly a 1% rise in broad stock indexes (1.3% in the Feb 2003 draft, which I read); most of the response comes from expected excess returns, not real rates or dividends. For cbtone: a sanity anchor for equity responses to rate surprises; our tone-surprise equity effects should not exceed this order of magnitude by more than 2x.

## Communication surveys and measurement

**4. Blinder, Ehrmann, Fratzscher, De Haan and Jansen (2008), `blinder2008central`.** *J. Economic Literature* 46(4):910-945. Question: what do theory and evidence say about central-bank communication? Data: survey of the literature covering Fed, ECB, BoE, BoJ and others up to 2008. Method: review of theory (expectations management, signalling) and empirics (market reaction, predictability, volatility). Finding: communication moves financial markets and improves predictability of decisions, but communication strategies differ widely and no consensus on the optimum exists. For cbtone: organising taxonomy (decisions, statements, minutes, speeches) and the reference list for what has and has not been tested outside the G3. No single effect size is reported; it is a survey.

**5. Ehrmann and Talmi (2020), `ehrmann2020starting`.** *J. Monetary Economics* 111:48-62 (BoC Staff WP 2016-37). Question: does textual similarity between consecutive statements affect market volatility? Data: 110 Bank of Canada press releases, 2001-2015; 1-year government bond yield, with robustness across rates, FX and equities. Method: cosine similarity between consecutive releases, regressed against event-window volatility with controls for surprise and tone. Finding: less similar releases raise volatility; a sharp rewording after a run of near-identical statements raises it substantially. Tone has explanatory power for the next decisions, ordered-probit pseudo R-squared up to 0.12. For cbtone: a text-distance feature we can reuse and a pseudo R-squared benchmark for tone-predicts-decision checks.

## Exchange-rate-based policy (MAS)

**6. Parrado (2004), `parrado2004singapore`.** IMF WP/04/10. Question: how does the Monetary Authority of Singapore conduct policy when the instrument is the trade-weighted exchange rate index (S$NEER) rather than a short rate? Data: Singapore macro data, quarterly, 1980s-early 2000s (period not verified in detail). Method: estimates a forward-looking reaction function with partial adjustment of the exchange rate index. Finding: inflation control is the principal objective; the rule responds to expected inflation and the output gap and reacts mainly to large deviations. No announcement or communication event study. For cbtone: the only well-sourced description of the MAS regime; it implies a "tone" signal should be tested against S$NEER or USD/SGD and not against a policy rate.

## Emerging-market and Asian central banks

**7. Ho and Karagedikli (2021), `ho2021effects`.** SEACEN WP2/2021 / CAMA WP 67/2021 (working paper; not confirmed as journal-published). Question: do Malaysian policy statements move yields beyond the rate decision? Data: Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM) statements, Aug 2005-Mar 2020 (T=97); daily changes in KLIBOR, MGS and swap yields; English statements. Method: Gürkaynak-style two-factor rotation. Finding: two factors are needed; the first (1-month KLIBOR surprise) explains about 71% of the one-year yield change; the path factor explains 53-74% of explainable variation in longer yields and affects corporate yields and spreads. On 8 May 2014 a hold moved 2- and 3-year MGS by +18 and +16 bp. For cbtone: closest design analogue for a non-inflation-targeting Asian bank; daily rather than intraday data, so R-squared values are not directly comparable to GSS.

**8. Hayo, Kutan and Neuenkirch (2012), `hayo2012federal`.** *Southern Economic J.* 78(3):1041-1056. Question: how do Fed actions and speeches affect emerging equity markets? Data: 17 emerging equity markets, 1998-2009, formal and informal Fed communication. Method: GARCH models with communication indices and target surprises. Finding: both actions and communications significantly affect returns, and target surprises are an important driver. Coefficient sizes were not retrieved. For cbtone: evidence that foreign-central-bank text matters for EM markets, relevant to a small open economy's exposure to Fed tone.

**9. Neuenkirch (2013), `neuenkirch2013transparency`.** *Economic Systems* 37(4):598-609. Question: does transparency improve market expectations of policy rates in emerging markets? Data: 25 emerging economies, Jan 1998-Dec 2009 (from the working-paper version, MAGKS 36-2011). Method: panel regressions of the gap between money-market rate and expected policy rate on the Eijffinger-Geraats transparency index. Finding: transparency reduces the bias; effects are larger without an exchange-rate peg and in low-income economies; intermediate transparency works best; political transparency matters most. For cbtone: the peg interaction bears directly on exchange-rate-centred regimes. No R-squared recorded.

**10. Filardo and Guinigundo (2008), `filardo2008transparency`.** BSP-BIS conference paper. Question: how transparent and communicative are Asia-Pacific central banks? Data: survey of Asian central-bank practices circa 2007-08. Method: descriptive comparison of communication practices, with a proposal to test effectiveness via the expectations hypothesis for the term structure. Finding: communication practices have expanded across the region and room for improvement remains. I read only the search-result description, not the full text; details of country coverage are unverified. For cbtone: background for what each Asian bank publishes, and a testable-implication framework.

**11. Montes and Nicolay (2016), `montes2016communication`.** *Análise Econômica* 34(66). Question: does Banco Central do Brasil communication shape inflation expectations? Data: Brazilian central bank communication and expectation surveys (inflation-targeting regime; Portuguese text; period not verified). Method: a fuzzy-set communication index related to inflation expectations. Finding: communication can worsen expectations and inflation when it signals risk of losing control of inflation (a "paradox of communication"). Taken from the abstract only. For cbtone: an emerging-market, non-English tone-index precedent; no basis-point effects reported.

## Multilingual / cross-lingual

**12. Silva, Moriya and Veyrune (2025), `silva2025text`.** IMF WP/25/109. Question: can LLMs classify central-bank communication consistently across banks and languages? Data: 74,882 multilingual documents from 169 central banks, 1884-2025. Method: fine-tuned LLM labels sentences by topic, stance, sentiment and audience. Finding: banks use neutral, fact-based language toward markets and signal risks more to governments; confidence-building rises in crises. Accuracy/F1 not found on the landing page. For cbtone: the main existing cross-lingual scorer to compare against; a discussant at an ECB 2026 conference reportedly raised context and multilingual-validity concerns (unread).

## Typical effect sizes for the "> 2x off literature" check

- Yield response to 1 pp funds-rate surprise (US, intraday): 2y +49 bp, 5y +28 bp, 10y +13 bp (GSS).
- Path-factor response to 1 pp move: 5y +37 bp, 10y +28 bp (GSS). Path share of explainable variation: 2/3 (2y), 3/4 (5y), 9/10 (10y).
- Equity: 25 bp unanticipated cut ≈ +1% to +1.3% (Bernanke-Kuttner); 1 pp tightening ≈ -4.3% (GSS).
- Event-study R-squared: S&P on target surprise .36 intraday vs .12 daily; T-bill .80 vs .56 (GSS). Malaysia: first factor ≈ 71% of 1-year yield variation; path factor 53-74% of explainable long-yield variation (Ho-Karagedikli).
- Tone predicting next decisions: ordered-probit pseudo R-squared up to 0.12 (Ehrmann-Talmi).

## Gaps

No located study measures MAS Monetary Policy Statement text against S$NEER or USD/SGD with an event window; the MAS entry is regime description only, and sell-side notes (not cited) are the sole commentary. No located study scores Thai-language and English statements side by side for any emerging bank; the Malaysian, Brazilian and Fed-to-EM evidence is monolingual per study. Combinations untested here: Asian banks x multilingual LLM scorer x intraday event study; exchange-rate-regime banks x text-similarity features; EM banks with minutes or speeches (only statements and press releases are covered); cross-lingual scorers (Silva et al.) validated against market reactions rather than human labels. Kuttner (2001) and Hayo et al. effect sizes were not read directly and should be re-checked before use as hard thresholds.
